# What Is Crohn’s Disease?

![https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/01/Crohn%27s_disease_-_colon_-_high_mag.jpg](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/01/Crohn%27s_disease_-_colon_-_high_mag.jpg)

Crohn's disease is one of the two main forms of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), the other being Ulcerative Colitis. It is an inflammatory condition that can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract, from the mouth to the anus. Crohn’s disease most commonly appears in individuals aged 15–30 years and in those over 65\.

The location of the disease varies: about 35% of cases occur in the Ileum, 45% involve ileocolitis, 20% are limited to the Colon, and less than 1% affect other locations. Unlike ulcerative colitis, inflammation in Crohn’s disease occurs in patchy, discontinuous sections separated by healthy tissue. Chronic inflammation may lead to complications such as colonic strictures, Fistula formation, and Abscess development, and it can potentially affect surrounding healthy tissue.

# Symptoms of Crohn’s Disease

The general symptoms of Crohn’s disease include diarrhea, weight loss, fatigue, fever, and abdominal pain or tenderness. However, some symptoms depend on which part of the intestine is affected. The ileum is the final section of the Small Intestine and helps absorb vitamins (especially Vitamin B12), minerals, and carbohydrates that are not absorbed in the Jejunum. When the ileum is affected, symptoms may include abdominal pain and cramping in the lower right area of the abdomen and nutrient deficiencies, particularly Vitamin B12 deficiency.

When Crohn’s disease affects both the ileum and colon, the condition is called ileocolitis. The ileum leads into the colon (large intestine) and represents the transition between nutrient absorption and waste removal. When this ileum–colon area is affected, abdominal pain in the middle or lower right area of the abdomen may occur, along with significant diarrhea. The colon, or large intestine, is responsible for removing water and some salts from food waste before it is expelled from the body. When the colon is affected, the area may become ulcerated and develop sores. In addition, the likelihood of developing Colon Cancer due to chronic inflammation may increase.

# Causes of Crohn’s Disease

The cause of Crohn’s disease, like many gastrointestinal diseases, is still unknown. However, researchers believe it may involve an Autoimmune disease reaction in which healthy cells in the intestinal lining are attacked. Smoking can also trigger the onset of Crohn’s disease by contributing to colon strictures and can worsen flare-ups in people who already have the disease. A high-fat diet may also influence the development of Crohn’s disease because large amounts of saturated fat can affect the gut barrier and alter the Gut Microbiome.

One possible cause of Crohn’s disease is Mycobacterium avium paratuberculosis (MAP). This bacterium causes a very similar disease called Johne's disease, a chronic intestinal disease found in ruminants such as cattle. Because Johne’s disease and Crohn’s disease have very similar symptoms and intestinal inflammation, researchers have investigated whether MAP may play a role in humans. MAP is often found in dairy cattle and can withstand many environmental conditions. Humans can become exposed to MAP through contaminated food or water, and the bacterium can sometimes resist processes such as pasteurization and chlorination. Studies have detected MAP in approximately 50–100% of Crohn’s patients. Although this evidence suggests a correlation between MAP infection and Crohn’s disease, finding MAP in these patients does not prove that it causes the disease, and more research is needed.

Genes can also play a role in developing Crohn’s disease. Some genes carry a higher risk. Individually, the effect is small, but together they can increase the likelihood of developing Crohn’s disease. Scientists have identified many genetic factors linked to Crohn’s disease, but these discoveries only explain about 13% of why people develop the condition. This means that most of the genetic influence is still unknown. The remaining genetic risk likely comes from common gene variants with very small effects and rare variants that have not yet been discovered. Genes with very large effects are rare, and most of them have already been identified. Finding the genes that increase risk is only the first step; researchers still need to determine how and why those genes contribute to Crohn’s disease.

Crohn’s disease is not caused by a single gene. Instead, it results from a combination of small genetic factors and environmental influences that increase a person’s risk, and scientists are still trying to understand the full picture.

# Diagnosis of Crohn’s Disease

![https://cdn2.picryl.com/photo/2018/04/20/lt-cmdr-brent-lacey-gastroenterologist-at-naval-ceefd7-1024.jpg](https://cdn2.picryl.com/photo/2018/04/20/lt-cmdr-brent-lacey-gastroenterologist-at-naval-ceefd7-1024.jpg)

Because Crohn’s disease is so diverse and unique to each person’s intestine, there is not one definitive way to test for it. Doctors will most likely first order blood and stool tests. A stool sample can show whether inflammation is present in the gut. If an abnormal level of the protein Calprotectin is detected, it may indicate that the disease is present. Anemia is also a common sign of Crohn’s disease and can be detected if there is a hemoglobin deficiency.

Endoscopic procedures are also common diagnostic tools and are often the second step in the diagnostic process. This can be done through an Endoscopy, where a camera is inserted into the esophagus, or through Capsule Endoscopy, where a capsule containing a camera is swallowed and images of the small intestine are taken. A Colonoscopy can also be performed, in which a small camera on a flexible tube is inserted into the anus to examine the entire colon or the end of the Ileum. During these procedures, a Biopsy from the gastrointestinal tract may be taken to detect Granuloma (clusters of white blood cells) or areas of inflammation.

A less common method of diagnosis involves imaging techniques. This can be done through a CT scan, where a contrast solution is ingested to examine the functioning of the bowels and reveal underlying structures. An MRI scan can also be used to detect abscesses or trauma in the anal or ileal region of the GI tract. These methods may be used to keep the process as minimally invasive as possible (for example, for small children) or if there is a risk of disturbing a Fistula or obstruction.

# Treatment of Crohn’s Disease

Crohn’s disease has several treatment options. One option is taking antibiotics, which may help reduce pain and inflammation and suppress the body’s immune response when it is triggered. Since this condition affects the Small Intestine, where nutrient absorption occurs, vitamin deficiencies are very common. Because of this, supplementing vitamins such as Vitamin D or Vitamin B12 may be necessary.

These vitamins can also be obtained through food; however, with Crohn’s disease, many foods that contain rich sources of vitamins can act as trigger foods. Nutrient-rich foods like dairy products and fatty meats may become less of an option. A food elimination diet may be necessary, with the most common foods removed being dairy and grains. Alcohol can also exacerbate symptoms.

The goal of treatment is to reduce inflammation and support healthy gut bacteria so that these foods may eventually become more tolerable. Smoking and alcohol can also trigger symptoms, so it is important to avoid them.
