## What Is Gluten? 

One of the most common misconceptions about gluten is that it’s a carbohydrate. While gluten is found in many carbohydrate-rich products such as breads and pastas, it’s actually considered a protein. Gluten can be found in any grains such as barley, rye, and oats with the exception of rice. It's made up of three components: Gliadin, Glutenin, and H2O which forms disulfide bonds after water is added. This is what gives gluten its sticky and stretchy properties. It’s because of gluten's properties that it acts like a binding agent in many of our foods. 

## What Is Celiac Disease? 

Celiac disease is an immune reaction against gluten. To best understand this disease, it's important to understand the pathology of celiac.  When gluten initially enters the small intestine (the site for protein absorption), the gluten protein is modified into a particular shape by an TG2 enzyme. The celiac gene is called HLA-DQ2 or DQ8–if this gene is found, you are likely susceptible to developing or having celiac disease. The now modified gluten protein will be able to fit onto the HLA protein molecule (I like to imagine it as having arms that are receiving the gluten protein.) Now normally, in individuals who do not carry the DQ2 or DQ8 gene, when the protein is handed off to the T-Helper cell to be inspected, it green lights it as harmless and recognizes it as gluten and therefore is able to be consumed and digested safely. But in individuals with the Celiac gene, the helper cells do not recognize the modified gluten as fluted and red lights it and marks it as a pathogen. When this is the case, the T-helper cell initiates an anti-inflammatory reaction to help the body. During this reaction, B-Cell begins to produce antibodies against not only the modified gluten, but also the TG2 enzyme. As long as the gluten is in the system, the immune reaction will persist, and begin to damage the surface area of the villi in the small intestine into stubs. The intestinal lining becomes so flat that it doesn't have enough surface for the nutrient area. The autoantibodies can travel throughout the body and therefore symptoms can take place anywhere. 

## Symptoms of Celiac Disease

The symptoms of celiac disease range from being asymptomatic to a variety of symptoms that differ in each individual. Some symptoms include skin rashes, intestinal pain or light-colored/ foul smelling diarrhea, anemia, weight loss and/or trouble gaining weight, and energy loss. A source will be attached detailing these symptoms and additional symptoms. One symptom every individual with celiac disease shares is damage to their small intestine.

## Causes of Celiac Disease

Research is still determining the cause of celiac disease. As of now, Celiac is most likely a gene that can be inherited from a “first degree” carrier such as a parent or close relative; this can influence the type of bacteria that grows in the infant's gut during development. It’s possible that over time an individual can become intolerant to gluten after consumption as aging occurs. Beginning of life factors such as breastfeeding, infections, and antibiotics can influence the bacteria that develops in the gut. The gut microbiome can train the immune system to recognize substances as harmful or safe, including gluten. But in someone with celiac, changes in gut bacteria, dysbiosis, can interfere with the recognition process as described above.

## Diagnoses of Celiac Disease

To diagnose celiac disease, your PCP most likely order a series of blood tests such as: Total IgA, tTG-IgA, EMA-IgA. IgA stands for Immunoglobulin A, which is an antibody that is mostly found in mucosal membranes (i.e. digestive system). It protects germs from invading healthy cells by trapping them in a thick mucus. If gluten is a normal part of your diet, and you're looking to get tested, do not remove gluten from your diet or else it will interfere with the accuracy of the results. Another common method of diagnosis is an endoscopy–which is usually if symptoms persist beyond the blood tests or results are unclear. During the endoscopy, the gastroenterologist will take a biopsy of the small intestine, and send it to the lab to confirm diagnoses. Because celiac is a hereditary disease, genetic testing can also be conducted to indicate the presence of HLA-DQ2 or DQ8.

## Treatments of Celiac Disease

A strict gluten-free diet is required to reduce inflammation and harmful symptoms after the consumption of gluten. A specialized dietician who works with individuals with digestive disorders is recommended to manage, and make healthy decisions with a restrictive diet. It’s important to be cautious that there is gluten in many nonfood items such as cosmetics or hygiene products. Nutritional deficiencies due to the restrictive diet may cause nutritional deficiencies, so vitamins or supplements may be prescribed. 

Researchers from Northwestern Medicine, led by Stephen Miller PhD, a professor in the Microbiology department at the Feinberg School of Medicine, have developed an experimental therapy that may reduce intolerance of gluten in individuals with celiac disease. In phase 2 of this clinical trial, those with celiac were given an IV nanoparticle treatment containing gluten inside a biodegradable shell. This nanoparticle named, “Trojan Horse”, is designed to signal  and train the immune system to not attack gluten. Patients receiving the IV treatment showed lower levels of inflammation after gluten compared to those receiving the placebo treatment. This advancement is being advanced with COUR Pharmaceuticals and Takeda, which has licensed technology for development and commercialization. This ground breaking design from Dr.Miller is a step towards not only treating celiac, but to also treating other autoimmune and allergic conditions.
